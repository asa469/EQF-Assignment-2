% ------------------------------------------------------------------ Abstract
\section*{Abstract}
This paper evaluates conditional volatility and correlation models for two London Stock Exchange ETFs
on the S\&P~500: \textbf{CSPX.L} (iShares, quoted in US dollars) and \textbf{VUAG.L} (Vanguard, quoted in
pounds), using $T=1{,}258$ daily returns from 2021 to 2025. GARCH(1,1) clearly beats EWMA and RiskMetrics,
and BIC selects GJR(1,1,1) from 36 GJR specifications for both funds. In this model negative shocks
drive volatility, with no measurable effect of positive ones. GJR reacted fastest to the Liberation Day tariff shock (2 April 2025) and, estimated
on 2021--2024, gives the best one-day-ahead variance forecasts for 2025. All Gaussian models leave
fat-tailed residuals; Student-$t$ errors ($\nu\approx5$) remove most of this misfit. A DCC model shows a
correlation between 0.44 and 0.93 that falls when the pound/dollar rate is volatile and rose to near its
sample maximum just after Liberation Day.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ 1. Introduction
\section{Introduction}
Volatility clustering is a well-known stylised fact of equity returns, and since volatility cannot be
observed it must be measured through a model. \citet[Chapter~9]{brooks2019} discusses several such
measures, from exponentially weighted moving averages to GARCH-type models. This paper asks which of them
works best in practice.

We compare GARCH(1,1), its restricted versions EWMA and RiskMetrics, and the asymmetric GJR model of
\citet{glosten1993}. We judge them by likelihood, information criteria, their reaction to the tariff
shock of April 2025, the distribution of their standardised residuals and, because in-sample fit can
flatter larger models, by the quality of their out-of-sample forecasts for 2025. As an extension, we study the
correlation between the two funds with the dynamic conditional correlation (DCC) model of
\citet{engle2002}.

Because both ETFs hold the S\&P~500 but are quoted in different currencies, their returns differ by the
pound/dollar exchange rate, so their correlation measures how much currency risk matters relative to
equity risk.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ 2. Data
\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Asset Selection Methodology}
From the LSE ETF universe of the first assignment we selected \textbf{CSPX.L} and \textbf{VUAG.L}
because they are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Benchmark funds:} large, liquid S\&P~500 funds, the US-dollar line of iShares and the
    sterling line of Vanguard. VUAG.L returns have a correlation of 0.993 with VUSA.L (Vanguard's
    distributing S\&P~500 line) but only 0.58 with FTSE~100 ETFs.
    \item \textbf{Accumulating:} dividends are reinvested, so the yearly dividend adjustment of this data
    set causes no jumps at year ends.
    \item \textbf{Plain physical funds:} leveraged and inverse products are avoided, because their daily
    resets distort the conditional variance.
    \item \textbf{Complete:} both have prices on all 1,259 trading days of 2021--2025; the 31 dates
    without prices are UK bank holidays and are dropped.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Returns and Descriptive Statistics}
We compute daily log-returns in percent, $y_t = 100\,(\ln P_t - \ln P_{t-1})$, which gives $T=1{,}258$
returns from 5 January 2021 to 30 December 2025, computed directly from the course price file
\texttt{etf\_full-L.csv}.

Since both funds hold the same index, the difference of their returns is approximately the change in the
exchange rate, $y^{\text{CSPX}}_t - y^{\text{VUAG}}_t \approx \Delta\ln(\text{USD per GBP})_t$. This
difference has a standard deviation of 0.54\% per day. Its largest values all occur during the UK
`mini-budget' crisis and the following sterling swings of autumn 2022, from $-3.05\%$ on 23 September to
$+2.67\%$ on 1 December 2022. The return correlation is 0.85 over the full
sample.

Table~\ref{tab:summary_stats} shows a small positive mean, negative skewness and excess kurtosis of about
3 for both funds. Normality is rejected, and the Ljung--Box test on squared returns shows strong
volatility clustering. VUAG.L is less volatile than CSPX.L (14.6\% against 16.1\% per year). The dollar
tends to strengthen when US equities fall: the correlation between the CSPX.L return and the currency
difference above is 0.44. For a sterling investor, the currency therefore softens equity losses.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ 3. Methodology
\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Baseline Volatility Models}
All models share the observation equation $y_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma_t^2)$ with $u_t = y_t - \mu$.
We use the notation of the assignment:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{GARCH(1,1)}:
    \begin{equation}
        \sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + \alpha u_t^2 + \delta \sigma_t^2 .
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA)}: the restriction $\omega = 0$,
    $\alpha = 1 - \delta$ gives
    \begin{equation}
        \sigma_{t+1}^2 = (1 - \delta)\, u_t^2 + \delta \sigma_t^2 ,
    \end{equation}
    with free parameters $\mu$ and $\delta$. The variance is integrated and has no long-run level.
    \item \textbf{RiskMetrics}: in addition $\delta = 0.94$ and $\mu = \bar y$, so nothing is optimised:
    \begin{equation}
        \sigma_{t+1}^2 = 0.06\, u_t^2 + 0.94\, \sigma_t^2 .
    \end{equation}
    We count $\mu$ as one parameter in the information criteria.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Advanced Model: GJR-GARCH}
As the advanced model we use the GJR model \citep{glosten1993},
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + \sum_{j=1}^{q}\alpha_j u^2_{t-j+1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_i\sigma^2_{t-i+1}
    + \sum_{k=1}^{o}\gamma_k u^2_{t-k+1}\,\mathbb{I}_{\{u_{t-k+1}<0\}},
\end{equation}
for all 36 combinations of $p\in\{0,1,2,3\}$, $q\in\{1,2,3\}$ and $o\in\{0,1,2\}$. With $\gamma_k>0$ a
negative return raises the next variance more than a positive return of the same size (the leverage
effect). GARCH(1,1) is the special case GJR(1,1,0).

As a robustness check we re-estimate the selected GJR model with Student-$t$ errors,
$u_t=\sigma_t\varepsilon_t$ with $\varepsilon_t\sqrt{\nu/(\nu-2)}\sim t_\nu$, so that $\varepsilon_t$ has
unit variance and $\nu>2$ measures the tail thickness. The normal model is the limit $\nu\to\infty$.

\subsection{Estimation and Model Comparison}
All models are estimated by maximum likelihood with the Gaussian log-likelihood
\begin{equation}
    \log L(\theta) = -\tfrac12\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(\ln 2\pi + \ln\sigma_t^2 + u_t^2/\sigma_t^2\Bigr).
\end{equation}
The recursion starts at $\sigma_1^2=T^{-1}\sum_t u_t^2$, and pre-sample values are set to the same value.
We impose $\omega>0$, $\alpha_j,\delta_i\ge0$, a non-negative response to negative shocks at each lag
($\alpha_k+\gamma_k\ge0$) and $\sum\alpha_j+\sum\delta_i+\tfrac12\sum\gamma_k<1$. The optimiser (SLSQP)
starts from several values, including the optimum of every model nested one order lower. This
guarantees that a larger model never ends up with a lower likelihood than a model nested in it, which we
verified for all 36 models. We work with returns in percent, because with raw returns $\omega$ is of
order $10^{-5}$, which makes the numerical optimisation badly scaled.

Standard errors come from a numerical Hessian. Normality is clearly rejected (Section~\ref{sec:diag}), so
we treat the estimates as quasi-maximum likelihood and report the robust sandwich standard errors of
\citet{bollerslev1992}. When a parameter lies on the boundary of the parameter space (such as
$\hat\alpha_1=0$ below), its standard error and the usual $\chi^2$ distribution of likelihood ratio (LR)
tests are not valid, and we interpret them with care.

To compare models we use LR tests for nested models (EWMA within GARCH, RiskMetrics within EWMA, GARCH
within GJR) and AIC and BIC for non-nested models. We also check whether the standardised residuals
$v_t = u_t/\sigma_t$ behave as the model implies.

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Forecast Evaluation}
We estimate every model once on 2021--2024 (1,007 returns), re-selecting the GJR order by BIC on this
period only, and keep the parameters fixed. Running the recursion through 2025 then gives one-day-ahead
forecasts $\sigma^2_t$ for the 251 trading days of 2025 that use only returns up to $t-1$. Since the true
variance is not observed, we use $y_t^2$ as proxy and evaluate the forecasts with the QLIKE loss
$\ln\sigma_t^2 + y_t^2/\sigma_t^2$ and the squared error $(y_t^2-\sigma_t^2)^2$. QLIKE is robust to the
noise in the proxy and penalises under-prediction more heavily \citep{patton2011}. Equal predictive accuracy
against GJR is tested with the \citet{diebold1995} test, using a Newey--West variance with five lags. We
also report the predictive log-likelihood and the share of days with $|u_t|>2\sigma_t$, which should be
about 4.6\% under normality.

\subsection{Dynamic Conditional Correlation (DCC)}
Using the standardised residuals $v_t=(v_{1t},v_{2t})'$ of the selected GJR models and
$S = \operatorname{var}(v)$, we estimate the correlation part of the DCC(1,1) model of
\citet{engle2002}:
\begin{align}
    Q_{t+1} &= S\,(1 - a - b) + a\, v_t v_t' + b\, Q_t , \qquad Q_1 = S,\\
    R_{t+1} &= (\operatorname{diag} Q_{t+1})^{-1/2}\, Q_{t+1}\, (\operatorname{diag} Q_{t+1})^{-1/2},
\end{align}
and maximise $\sum_t \log\phi_2(v_t; 0, R_t)$ over $(a,b)$. Here $a$ measures the reaction of the
correlation to new shocks and $b$ its persistence. The correlation $R_t$ used to evaluate $v_t$ depends
only on $v_1,\dots,v_{t-1}$ (given $S$ and the first-step GJR estimates, which use the full sample).

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ 4. Results
\section{Univariate Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Choice of the GJR Model}
Table~\ref{tab:grid_top} lists the five best GJR models by BIC; Table~\ref{tab:grid} in the appendix gives
all 36. The lagged variance is
the most important ingredient. Without it ($p=0$, ARCH models) the fit is much worse, and even three
ARCH lags fall far short of a single $\delta_1$. The asymmetry term comes next: moving from $o=0$ to
$o=1$ raises the log-likelihood by about 34 (CSPX.L) and 32 (VUAG.L) points. Additional ARCH lags
($q>1$) add almost nothing. Once the asymmetry term is included their estimates go to zero, so many of
the larger models reproduce the likelihood of a smaller one and are penalised for the extra parameters.

BIC selects GJR(1,1,1) for both series. AIC prefers GJR(2,1,1) for CSPX.L and GJR(3,1,2) for VUAG.L, but
only by 0.2 points. LR tests do not reject GJR(1,1,1) against these models ($p=0.14$ and $p=0.10$). We
therefore use GJR(1,1,1) for both series, which also gives one specification for the DCC model.

\subsection{Estimation Results and Model Selection}
Table~\ref{tab:params} reports the estimates, robust standard errors, log-likelihoods and information
criteria of the four models, and Table~\ref{tab:lr} the LR tests.

The mean return is about 0.05--0.09\% per day. For GARCH(1,1), $\hat\alpha=0.13$ (CSPX.L) and $0.10$
(VUAG.L) and $\hat\delta=0.83$ and $0.86$. Persistence is about 0.96, so a shock to the variance has a
half-life of about 15 trading days. The implied unconditional variance
$\hat\omega/(1-\hat\alpha-\hat\delta)$ equals 1.04 and 0.86, close to the sample variances.

The EWMA estimates of $\delta$ are 0.946 and 0.952, close to the RiskMetrics value 0.94, and the LR test
does not reject RiskMetrics against EWMA. The restriction that turns GARCH into EWMA, however, is
strongly rejected (LR of 47 and 46 with two degrees of freedom). The data prefer a variance that reverts
to a long-run level and reacts more strongly to new shocks.

For both assets \textbf{GJR(1,1,1)} gives the highest log-likelihood and the lowest AIC and BIC. Its
$\hat\alpha_1$ is zero in both series, while $\hat\gamma_1=0.22$ and $0.19$ with robust standard errors of
0.05. Volatility is thus driven by falling prices, and the data show no measurable effect of positive
returns, a strong form of the leverage effect. Because $\hat\alpha_1$ lies on the boundary its standard
error is not reliable, and a small positive $\alpha_1$ cannot be ruled out. Still, the asymmetry is convincing: GJR improves on GARCH(1,1) by about 34 log-likelihood
points for one extra parameter.

Most parameters are precisely estimated. The exception is $\omega$ for CSPX.L (robust $t\approx1.6$).

The last column re-estimates GJR(1,1,1) with Student-$t$ errors. The estimated degrees of freedom are
$\hat\nu=5.3$ and $5.2$, far from the normal limit, and the log-likelihood rises by 31 and 38 points for
one extra parameter. The variance dynamics hardly change ($\hat\alpha_1=0$, $\hat\delta_1=0.84$,
$\hat\gamma_1=0.27$ and $0.21$), so the asymmetry is not an artefact of the normal assumption. For CSPX.L
the robust standard errors of the $t$ model are large (for example 0.29 for $\hat\delta_1$, against 0.09
non-robust). The large gap between the two suggests that the $t$ model is still misspecified, plausibly
because of the negative skewness discussed in Section~\ref{sec:diag}.

\subsection{Full Sample Volatility Dynamics}
Figure~\ref{fig:vol_dynamics} plots the full-sample $2\sigma_t$ bands of the four models against the
absolute returns $|y_t|$. All models trace the main regimes: the 2022 bear market, the calm of
2023--2024, the short spike of August 2024 and the tariff shock of 2025. They differ in timing and
amplitude. GJR has the sharpest peaks after market falls, and in rising markets it often gives the lowest
volatility. EWMA and RiskMetrics are the smoothest. They react weakly to a single large return
($\alpha\approx0.05$), but stay elevated for a long time because they lack mean reversion. GARCH lies in
between.

\subsection{Reaction to the Tariff Shock around Liberation Day (April 2, 2025)}
The tariffs were announced on 2 April 2025, after the London close. Figure~\ref{fig:shock_reaction} zooms
in on this period. For the first London return after the announcement (3 April) all models predicted
$2\sigma_t\approx2.1$--$2.3\%$, but the returns were $-3.8\%$ (CSPX.L) and $-4.9\%$ (VUAG.L). VUAG.L fell
more because the dollar weakened as well, so here the dollar did not act as a safe haven.

The next day GJR raised $2\sigma_t$ to 4.2\% and 4.8\%, GARCH to 3.5\% and 3.7\%, and EWMA only to 2.8\%
and 3.0\%. GJR peaked at 6.7\% and 5.8\% within a week, close to the realised absolute returns of 4--5\%;
EWMA and RiskMetrics peaked at only 4.2--5.1\%, and only on 11 April. The rebound of up to 5\% after the
tariff pause (9 April) barely moves GJR, whose volatility falls from 11 April while GARCH still rises. By
early May GJR is back near its pre-shock level, whereas EWMA and RiskMetrics still give
$2\sigma_t\approx3.6\%$. GJR thus captured both the build-up and the decay of the shock best.

\subsection{Residual Diagnostics via Normal Quantile Transform}\label{sec:diag}
If a model is correctly specified, $v_t = u_t/\sigma_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and the normal quantile
transform $q_t = \Phi(v_t)$ is uniform on $(0,1)$. (We use $\Phi$, the probability integral transform;
$\Phi^{-1}$ as written in the assignment would map to the real line instead.) For the Student-$t$ model
$\Phi$ is replaced by the cdf of the standardised $t_{\hat\nu}$ distribution.
Figure~\ref{fig:quantile_hist} shows the histograms of $q_t$, and Table~\ref{tab:diag} reports formal
tests.

The volatility dynamics are captured well. No model leaves significant autocorrelation in $v_t^2$, and
GARCH and GJR give $v_t$ a standard deviation of 1.00. EWMA and RiskMetrics give 1.04--1.05, because
their variance is not tied to the level of the data. The shape of the distribution, however, is not
normal. The histograms have too many observations in the middle and in the outermost
bins and too few at moderate quantiles. This is the signature of fatter tails than the normal. The excess
kurtosis of $v_t$ is 1.3--2.6, and both the Kolmogorov--Smirnov and the $\chi^2$ test reject uniformity
for every Gaussian model. The lowest bin is higher than the highest one, reflecting the remaining
negative skewness. GJR reduces the kurtosis most (1.34 and 1.77) but does not remove it.

With Student-$t$ errors the histograms become nearly flat (last column of Figure~\ref{fig:quantile_hist}).
The KS test no longer rejects ($p=0.40$ and $0.94$), and for VUAG.L neither does the $\chi^2$ test
($p=0.43$). For CSPX.L the $\chi^2$ test still rejects ($p=0.002$): the lowest bin is too full, because the
symmetric $t$ cannot capture the skewness of $-0.5$. The fat tails are therefore the main failure of the
Gaussian models; a skewed-$t$ distribution would be the natural next step.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ Forecasts
\section{Out-of-Sample Forecasts for 2025}\label{sec:forecast}
Estimated on 2021--2024 alone, BIC again selects GJR(1,1,1) for both series, with $\hat\alpha_1=0$ and
$\hat\gamma_1\approx0.2$, so the model choice is stable over time. Table~\ref{tab:forecast} evaluates the
forecasts for 2025.

The ranking of Section~\ref{sec:results} holds out of sample. GJR(1,1,1) has the lowest QLIKE for both
series, followed by GARCH(1,1), RiskMetrics and EWMA. The Diebold--Mariano test shows that GJR is
significantly better than EWMA and RiskMetrics for both funds ($p\le0.02$), and than GARCH for CSPX.L
($p=0.02$), but not for VUAG.L ($p=0.40$). April 2025 contributes a large share of the gain, but not all
of it: without that month the QLIKE differences shrink, yet EWMA and RiskMetrics remain worse at about the
5\% level. The squared
error ranks the models in the same order, but no difference is significant. This loss is dominated by a
few days with very large $y_t^2$, so with one year of data it has little power.

Coverage tells the same story. EWMA puts $|u_t|$ outside $2\sigma_t$ on 8.0\% of the days for both funds,
significantly more than the nominal 4.6\% (binomial $p=0.015$): its slow reaction leaves it with too
low a variance when a shock starts. GJR (4.4\% and 5.2\%) and GARCH (4.8\% and 6.0\%) are close to the
nominal rate.

The Student-$t$ model gives practically the same variance forecasts as the normal GJR (QLIKE and MSE do not
differ significantly), but a much higher predictive log-likelihood ($+9$ and $+14$). Its density forecasts
are better because they put more mass in the tails. The choice between the two thus depends on the use:
for a volatility forecast the normal QMLE is enough, for risk measures such as value-at-risk the $t$
distribution is preferable.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ 5. Correlation
\section{Extra: Correlation and DCC Analysis}\label{sec:corr}

\subsection{Sub-Sample Correlations}
Table~\ref{tab:corr_breakdown} reports the correlation between the standardised GJR(1,1,1) residuals over
the full sample and its three thirds. The full-sample correlation is 0.81. It falls from 0.85 in the first
third to 0.74 in the second third (September 2022 -- May 2024) and recovers to 0.84 in the last third.
With standard errors of 0.01--0.02 these differences are large, and a Fisher-$z$ test rejects equal
correlations ($\chi^2_2=22.7$, $p<0.001$), so the correlation is not stable.

The currency explains this pattern. The second third contains the sterling crisis of autumn 2022 and the
large dollar moves of 2023. The standard deviation of the return difference is 0.65\% there, against
0.51\% and 0.43\% in the other thirds, and equity volatility was lower than in the first third. With
relatively more currency noise, the two returns move less closely together.

\subsection{DCC Estimates}
The maximum likelihood DCC estimates are $\hat a=0.052$ (0.014) and $\hat b=0.920$ (0.021)
(Table~\ref{tab:dcc}), so both parameters are precisely estimated. The standard errors are robust
sandwich errors. They ignore the estimation error of the first-step GJR models and are therefore
somewhat too small. The persistence $\hat a+\hat b=0.972$ implies a half-life of about 25 trading days
for a shock to the correlation. DCC improves the log-likelihood by 42 points over a constant correlation.
Under the null of constant correlation $b$ is not identified, so the LR statistic of 84.8 has no standard
distribution, but it is far too large to be explained by chance.

Figure~\ref{fig:dcc_plot} plots $\rho_t$ together with a 63-day rolling correlation as a model-free

deep troughs coincide with currency turmoil: the sterling swings in the months after the September 2022
mini-budget ($\rho_t\approx0.54$ in November) and the sharp fall of the dollar after the Federal Reserve signalled
rate cuts in December 2023 (the minimum of 0.44). At such times the two funds behave noticeably
differently, even though they hold the same stocks.

Around Liberation Day the correlation did not fall but rose, from 0.90 on 2 April to 0.93 on 4 April,
just below the sample maximum (0.932 in January 2022), and it stayed at about 0.90 or higher for the rest
of April. The equity shock was
so large that it dominated the currency moves, and both standardised residuals were large and of the same
sign on several consecutive days. Correlation thus increased exactly when diversification between the two
currency lines would have been most valuable, as is typical in market-wide crises.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ 6. Conclusion
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Our main findings are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{GARCH(1,1)} clearly beats EWMA and RiskMetrics. The data reject $\omega=0$ and
    $\alpha+\delta=1$, and the integrated models react too little to large shocks and stay elevated for
    too long afterwards. RiskMetrics, despite having no estimated parameters, is almost as good as EWMA,
    since $\hat\delta\approx0.94$.
    \item \textbf{GJR(1,1,1)}, selected by BIC from 36 specifications, improves substantially on
    GARCH(1,1). In this model volatility is driven by negative returns, and it followed the
    Liberation Day shock most closely. It also gives the best out-of-sample variance forecasts for 2025,
    significantly better than EWMA and RiskMetrics.
    \item All models capture the volatility clustering, but the Gaussian models leave fat-tailed residuals:
    their $q_t$ are not uniform. Student-$t$ errors ($\hat\nu\approx5$) largely fix this and give
    better density forecasts; only the skewness of CSPX.L remains.
    \item \textbf{DCC(1,1)} shows a high but clearly time-varying correlation ($a+b=0.97$). The
    correlation is driven by pound/dollar volatility and rose during the tariff crash.
\end{enumerate}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ 7. Limitations
\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Distributional assumptions:} Student-$t$ errors capture the fat tails, but not the
    negative skewness of CSPX.L; a skewed-$t$ distribution would address this.
    \item \textbf{Forecast design:} the parameters are estimated once and one year of forecasts, dominated
    by April 2025, is a short evaluation sample. Rolling re-estimation and a longer period would give more
    power, especially for the MSE loss.
    \item \textbf{Two-step DCC inference:} the DCC standard errors ignore the estimation error of the GJR
    step and are somewhat too small.
    \item \textbf{Variance proxy:} $y_t^2$ is a noisy proxy for the daily variance. Intraday realised
    volatility would sharpen both the forecast comparison and the analysis of announcement days.
\end{enumerate}
